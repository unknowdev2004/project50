            Related Tools

            How to Calculate Study Hours

            The simplest planning question is also the most useful one: how many hours do I need to study each day? Required hours per day is your total remaining workload divided by the number of days you actually intend to study. If you have 60 hours of material and 15 study days, that's 4 hours a day — no more, no less.

            Once you add a maximum daily capacity, the calculator compares your required pace against what you can realistically fit in, and tells you whether you're on track, cutting it close, or facing a workload that doesn't fit your available time.

            How to Make a Study Plan Before an Exam

            Exam Planner turns a start date, an exam date, your subjects, and the hours you have each day into an actual day-by-day schedule. It only ever schedules the study days you select, never exceeds the hours you have available, and never schedules past your exam date.

            Each day's time is split into new study, revision, and a small buffer, and the highest-priority incomplete subject is scheduled first — but the planner rotates between subjects with similar priority so you're not stuck on one subject for a week straight.

            How to Plan Your Syllabus Before Exams

            Syllabus Planner works in topics rather than hours. For each subject, enter how many topics remain and how long each one typically takes you — the tool multiplies these to find your total remaining workload, then divides by your available study days to find both an hours-per-day and a topics-per-day pace.

            This view is useful earlier in a term, when you're thinking in "chapters left" rather than "hours left", and want a simple pace target you can track week to week.

            How to Create a Catch-Up Study Plan

            Falling behind doesn't need a new formula — it needs the same one, recalculated with today's numbers. Catch-Up mode takes your remaining workload, remaining days, and available hours per day, and shows you the new required pace and exactly how far short your current availability falls.

            Where the gap is real, the tool lays out three concrete alternatives — more hours per day, more study days, or less workload — and the shortfall each one would need to close, so you can choose what actually fits your situation.

            How to Allocate Revision Time

            Revision competes with new study for the same hours, so Exam Planner treats it as a split of your available time rather than extra time added on top. With more than seven days left, the default is 70% new study and 30% revision; with three to seven days, it's an even 50/50; with one or two days left, revision takes over at 80%, since there's little value in starting brand-new material the night before an exam.

            These are sensible starting defaults, not fixed rules — you can override the percentage directly in Advanced Planning Options if your subjects need a different balance.

            Why Buffer Time Helps

            A study plan built around 100% of your available hours has no room for a bad day, a slower topic, or an unplanned interruption — one missed session and the whole schedule falls apart. Buffer time solves this by reserving a slice of your available capacity, 10% by default, before anything else is allocated.

            If you have 4 available hours and a 10% buffer, 24 minutes are set aside and the remaining 3 hours 36 minutes are split between new study and revision. It's a small sacrifice that makes the rest of the plan much more resilient to real life.

            How to Prioritize Difficult Subjects

            Difficulty and priority never change how many hours a subject needs — they only change the order subjects get scheduled in. A 10-hour subject stays a 10-hour subject whether it's marked Easy or Hard; marking it Hard simply moves it earlier in the queue so it isn't left until the last few days.

            Behind the scenes, each subject gets a planning score based on its share of your total workload, multiplied by its difficulty weight and priority weight. It's used only to decide scheduling order — it's never shown as a mysterious "AI score", and it never inflates the hours you originally estimated.

            Common Study Planning Mistakes

            Mistake 1 — Planning against calendar days instead of real study days. If you only study on weekdays, using every calendar day between now and your exam overstates your capacity. Select your actual study days in Exam Planner so the pace reflects reality.

            Mistake 2 — Treating estimated hours as flexible. Underestimating a subject's remaining hours to make a plan "fit" doesn't change the real workload — it just means the plan quietly fails later.

            Mistake 3 — Skipping revision entirely. A plan built only around new material leaves nothing to reinforce it. Even a 70/30 split protects some time for review from day one.

            Mistake 4 — Ignoring a "Not Realistic" result. If the required pace exceeds your available time, adding motivation won't close that gap — only more days, more hours, or less workload will. Use Catch-Up mode to see the real options.
