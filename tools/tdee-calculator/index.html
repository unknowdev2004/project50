About This Tool

This TDEE Calculator answers the most fundamental question in nutrition: how many calories should I eat? It calculates your Total Daily Energy Expenditure — the total calories your body burns each day — and generates personalised calorie targets for every goal, from aggressive fat loss to muscle gain.

Unlike tools that only show your maintenance number, this calculator shows a complete picture: five goal targets, expected monthly weight change, a daily meal breakdown, and how your maintenance calories shift across activity levels. Everything you need to make an informed decision about your calorie intake in one place.

All calculations use the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, which is widely regarded as the most accurate BMR formula for most adults. Safety floors are enforced — 1,500 kcal for men and 1,200 kcal for women — to prevent dangerously low targets. No data is ever sent to a server. Your inputs are saved locally in your browser.

How To Use

  1. Select your gender — this affects the BMR constant in the Mifflin-St Jeor equation.

  2. Enter your age in years (18 or older).

  3. Select your preferred height unit and enter your height. Values convert automatically when you switch units.

  4. Select your preferred weight unit and enter your current body weight. Values convert automatically.

  5. Choose your activity level — this is the biggest variable in the calculation. Most people should choose Sedentary or Lightly Active. Be honest.

  6. Click Calculate TDEE to see your calorie targets, monthly projections, meal breakdown and more.

What Is TDEE?

TDEE stands for Total Daily Energy Expenditure — the total number of calories your body burns in a single day. It accounts for everything: the energy needed to keep you alive at rest (BMR), the energy used to digest food (TEF), and the energy burned through all movement — from structured exercise to simply walking around your home.

TDEE is the number that determines your body weight. Eat at TDEE and you maintain. Eat below and you lose. Eat above and you gain. Knowing your TDEE is therefore the starting point for any rational approach to changing your body composition.

How TDEE Is Calculated

TDEE = BMR × Activity Multiplier. First, your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) is calculated using the Mifflin-St Jeor equation:

  • Male BMR: (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) + 5
  • Female BMR: (10 × weight in kg) + (6.25 × height in cm) − (5 × age) − 161

BMR is then multiplied by an activity factor: Sedentary (1.2), Lightly Active (1.375), Moderately Active (1.55), Very Active (1.725), Athlete (1.9). The result is your TDEE — your estimated maintenance calories.

Calories for Different Goals

Once your TDEE is known, goal calorie targets are simple adjustments:

  • Maintain Weight: Eat at TDEE.
  • Lose Fat: TDEE − 500 kcal/day → approximately 1.95 kg/month fat loss.
  • Aggressive Fat Loss: TDEE − 750 kcal/day → approximately 2.92 kg/month. More demanding to sustain.
  • Lean Muscle Gain: TDEE + 300 kcal/day → approximately 1.17 kg/month lean gain (mostly muscle with minimal fat).
  • Weight Gain: TDEE + 500 kcal/day → approximately 1.95 kg/month (faster mass gain with some fat).

Monthly change is estimated using 7,700 kcal per kg of body tissue. Individual results vary.

Factors That Affect Daily Calorie Needs

Several factors influence how many calories you need each day beyond what any formula can capture:

  • Muscle mass: Muscle is metabolically active — more muscle means a higher BMR even at rest.
  • Non-exercise activity (NEAT): Fidgeting, walking, standing and incidental movement can vary by up to 800 kcal/day between individuals at the same activity level.
  • Age: Metabolic rate tends to decline slightly with age, partly due to loss of muscle mass.
  • Sleep quality: Poor sleep raises ghrelin (hunger hormone) and lowers leptin (satiety hormone), leading to higher effective calorie needs to feel satisfied.
  • Hormonal status: Thyroid hormones, cortisol and sex hormones all influence how efficiently your body uses energy.
  • Diet history: Long-term calorie restriction can lower metabolic rate through adaptive thermogenesis — the body's response to perceived famine.
