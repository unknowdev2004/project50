About This Tool

The Ideal Weight Calculator helps you understand the weight range considered healthy for your height and gender. Rather than producing a single number, it computes four separate formula results and synthesises them into a minimum, recommended, and maximum weight — giving you a realistic, evidence-informed target range.

This tool uses four of the most widely cited ideal-weight formulas: Devine (1974), Hamwi (1964), Robinson (1983), and Miller (1983). Each formula was derived from clinical or population data and has been used in medical settings for decades. The consensus approach used here reduces the potential error of any single formula and gives a balanced result.

The results are most relevant for adults of average build. If you have a particularly high muscle mass or unusual body composition, the numbers may not fully represent your healthy weight. Use the range as a general guide and consult a healthcare provider for personalised advice.

How To Use This Calculator

  1. Select your gender — the formulas adjust their base values depending on biological sex, so this affects the output significantly.

  2. Enter your age. While the formulas themselves do not change with age, age helps contextualise your results and is stored for future use.

  3. Select your preferred height unit (cm or ft/in) and enter your height. Values convert automatically when you switch units, so there is no need to recalculate.

  4. Select your preferred weight unit (kg or lbs) and enter your current body weight. This is used to compute your current status relative to the healthy range.

  5. Click Calculate Ideal Weight to see your minimum, recommended, and maximum weights, your position on the weight indicator bar, your current status, and a personalised next step.

What Is An Ideal Weight?

There is no single perfect weight. The concept of an "ideal" body weight emerged in the mid-20th century as a clinical shorthand for the weight associated with the lowest risk of disease or the best health outcomes for a given height and sex. Over time, several researchers published formulas based on large samples of patients or populations.

What these formulas all agree on is that healthy weight exists within a range, not at a precise point. Two people of the same height can be equally healthy at weights 5–10 kg apart, depending on their body composition, fitness level, age, and genetics. Thinking about weight as a range rather than a single number is both more accurate and less stressful.

Healthy Weight Range vs Ideal Weight

Many calculators present a single "ideal weight" number, which implies a level of precision that the underlying science does not support. When four established formulas give results of 68, 69, 70, and 71 kg for the same person, which one is correct? All of them — within their respective margins of error.

Project 50 shows a range rather than one number because this is more honest and more useful. The minimum weight is the lowest of the four formula results; the maximum is the highest; and the recommended weight is the average across all four. This approach acknowledges the variability between formulas and gives you a zone to aim for rather than a single, potentially misleading target.

Factors Affecting Healthy Weight

Height is the primary driver of all four formulas used here. Every formula starts from a base weight at a reference height and adjusts proportionally. Even small differences in height produce noticeably different healthy weight ranges.

Gender significantly affects the result. Female bodies naturally carry more essential fat than male bodies (approximately 10–13% vs 2–5%), which means the formulas produce lower baseline weights for males. This is built into all four formulas.

Muscle mass is not directly accounted for by any ideal-weight formula. A person who lifts weights regularly may have a body weight above the calculated range while carrying very little fat. For such individuals, the range is better understood as a minimum healthy weight rather than an upper limit.

Body composition — the ratio of fat to lean tissue — matters more to health outcomes than total weight alone. Two people at the same weight can have very different health profiles depending on how much of that weight is muscle versus fat. The calculator gives a useful starting point, but body composition tools such as the Body Fat Calculator offer additional insight.

Understanding Your Results

Minimum Weight is the lowest result across the four formulas. Falling below this weight suggests you may be underweight for your height and could benefit from gradual, nutritionally adequate weight gain.

Recommended Weight is the average of all four formula results. This is the centre of your healthy range and the most balanced target to aim for if you are working toward a healthier weight.

Maximum Weight is the highest formula result. Weights above this level may indicate excess body fat relative to height, though muscle mass can legitimately push some individuals above this figure.

Current Status tells you whether your current weight falls below the healthy range, within it, or above it. This is calculated by comparing your entered weight against the minimum and maximum formula results.

Weight Difference shows how far your current weight is from the healthy range. If you are within the range, the difference is zero and the status reads "Already Within Healthy Range." Otherwise, the figure shows how much weight to gain or lose to enter the range.
